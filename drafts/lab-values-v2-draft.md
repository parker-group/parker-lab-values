# Parker Group Values and Expectations

This is a living document describing the values, expectations, and general culture of the Parker Group. It is written primarily for PhD students whom Daniel advises or co-advises, although much of it applies more broadly to people working with the group.

The Parker Group changes as people join, graduate, move on, and develop new interests. This document should change with it. It should reflect the people who make up the group and what we have learned about doing good work together.

At the same time, some principles are foundational to the kind of lab Daniel is willing to run and should persist across different generations of the group. Other things are expectations that follow from those values. Still others are simply advice: things that people in and around the group have found useful for becoming productive, independent scholars.

The document therefore moves from the most foundational principles of the Parker Group, through expectations for working together, to advice and practices that may or may not work equally well for everyone.

---

# I. Our Values

## Do good work

Do good work.

High-quality research doesn't require publication in prestigious journals like *Lancet*, *Nature*, *Science*, or *Cell*. What matters most is that your work is logical, sound, thoughtfully designed, and useful for answering meaningful questions, even simple ones.

Prioritize rigor over speed. Research outputs matter for your degree, career, collaborators, and the lab, but it is far better to delay a presentation or publication if the methods and results are not yet solid.

Know your work. Even when other people help develop your methods or analysis, you should be able to explain and defend the decisions, methods, and results that bear your name.

If you can't explain it, take the time to figure it out.

## Be ambitious about the work, not about defeating other people

Academia is competitive. Jobs, grants, publications, awards, and attention are limited, and there is nothing wrong with wanting to succeed.

But don't make outperforming other people the primary motivation for your work.

Care about the questions and problems you are working on. Become very good at answering them. Produce excellent work. Help the people around you produce excellent work.

Within the Parker Group, collaboration is an expectation, not simply a preference. There's no competition within the group, but always try your best. We don't need someone else to fail for us to succeed. Share data, code, protocols, ideas, opportunities, and expertise when appropriate. Help one another.

More broadly, try not to turn colleagues outside the group into opponents either. The best global health work comes from teams, often very large and diverse ones.

Part of what we are trying to demonstrate is that a kinder, more thoughtful, and collaborative scientific process can produce excellent science without reproducing the hyper-competitive and ruthless parts of academic culture.

That only works if the work is good.

Do good work and let the reputation follow.

## Be collegial

Be welcoming to one another and proactive in opening up discussions or offering assistance.

Share and celebrate achievements. Talk about failures too. Both are part of doing science.

Be open to giving and receiving feedback.

Being direct is fine, but always be kind.

Remember: sharing is caring.

## Be curious, careful, and intellectually honest

Engage with people and ideas. Ask questions. Go to talks. Talk to people whose work is different from yours. Be interested in what other people know.

Be careful about what you claim to know. There is nothing wrong with saying "I don't know."

Learn to distinguish among:

- what you know;
- what the evidence suggests;
- what you think or hypothesize might be true; and
- what you simply don't know yet.

Scientific credibility takes a long time to build and can be damaged surprisingly quickly. When in doubt, check.

## Your reputation matters (and so does the reputation of the Parker Group)

Build your reputation through good work and good interactions, not by telling people how impressive you or the lab are.

Engage. Ask questions. Be thoughtful, generous, and interested in other people's work. Follow through on things you say you will do. Give credit generously and appropriately.

Be particularly careful about what you present as fact, including when talking about the work of the Parker Group. Very few individual members know all of the research happening across the group, including the many projects Daniel works on with collaborators in different places. Don't assume that because you know one part of the lab's work, you can accurately describe all of it.

If you aren't sure, don't guess. It is perfectly fine to say that you don't know.

A good reputation is built through repeated interactions: doing good work, knowing what you are talking about, being interested in other people's work, following through, and being someone other people want to work with.

## Diversity makes our science better

Global health research has historically been dominated by people from wealthy countries and institutions, often working in places and with populations very different from their own.

We work across countries, disciplines, cultures, and methodological traditions. We should actively seek out perspectives and expertise different from our own.

People with different backgrounds, lived experiences, disciplinary training, methodological expertise, and ways of thinking will see things that you do not. This is particularly important in global health, where assuming that your own perspective is universal can lead to bad science.

Surround yourself with people who approach problems differently. Listen to local expertise. Take different forms of knowledge seriously.

Diverse perspectives make our work better.

# II. What We Expect of Each Other

## Take ownership

The Parker Group generally operates with substantial autonomy. Daniel does not want to micromanage your schedule, dictate every research question, or continually chase you for updates.

That freedom comes with responsibility.

Take ownership of your work. Keep projects moving. Communicate. Follow through. Ask for help when you need it.

As you progress through a PhD, you should become increasingly capable of identifying what needs to happen next and making it happen.

The goal is to become an independent scholar. This is your apprenticeship.

## Be productive

Hard work is essential, but in academia what you accomplish matters more than how long you spend at your desk.

For PhD students, peer-reviewed papers should be the primary research output you work toward. Proposals come next: they support future work, sharpen ideas, and learning to formulate fundable research is part of becoming an independent scholar. Presentations matter too, with oral presentations generally prioritized over posters.

Posters can be particularly useful early in a PhD, while you are developing a project and learning to communicate your work. As you progress, the emphasis should increasingly shift toward papers, proposals, and oral presentations.

None of this means that everything useful produces an immediate output. Fieldwork, data collection, learning difficult methods, developing collaborations, reading, thinking, and simply figuring things out take time. But over the longer term, activity should lead somewhere.

Your CV ultimately reflects outputs and accomplishments, not the number of hours you worked.

If you are working hard but struggling to translate that effort into finished work, let's strategize together.

## Participate

A collaborative lab only works if people participate in it.

Come to lab meetings when you are expected to be there. Engage with other people's presentations and ideas. Ask questions. Offer useful feedback. Share your own work, including work that is unfinished.

If you know you will miss something important, communicate that.

Participation is not simply attendance. Help make the group useful for other people.

## Manage your mentoring relationships

Success in research requires a team of mentors.

No single mentor (yes, including Daniel!) can provide everything you will need. Seek out people with different experiences, perspectives, and skill sets.

Mentorship doesn't always come with formal titles. Give appropriate recognition and credit to the people who actually contribute to your development and your work. Be mindful of "ghost mentorship," where people provide substantial intellectual or professional mentorship but receive little recognition for doing so.

Take responsibility for maintaining your mentoring relationships. Keep mentors informed about what you are doing and be proactive about arranging meetings and asking for feedback.

This is particularly important when you are co-advised. Different mentors may have different expectations, priorities, and working styles. Don't silently try to navigate incompatible expectations. Communicate with your mentors and surface conflicts when they arise.

Your mentors should not routinely have to chase you to find out what you are doing.

## Contribute to other people's success

Collaboration requires contribution.

Read drafts. Share useful resources. Help troubleshoot code or methods when you can. Connect people who should know one another. Share opportunities. Celebrate other people's successes.

You do not need to know everything or help with everything. But everyone should contribute something to making the group better.

# III. Some Advice for Doing Good Work

These are suggestions, not rules. Different people work differently. Keep what helps you, discard what doesn't, and add to this list when you discover something worth passing on.

## Learn how you work

Identify when your brain works best and structure your time accordingly.

For example, Daniel is most creative in the early morning and tries to reserve that time for writing.

As you progress through your PhD, you will increasingly have control over your time. Use it wisely.

Protect time for the things that require sustained thought.

## Finish things

Ideas are easy to accumulate. Finished work is harder.

Try not to let exciting new ideas continually displace work that is already underway. Learn when something needs more thought and when it simply needs to be finished.

Research outputs snowball over time. Finishing one good piece of work often creates the foundation for the next one.

## Learn how to learn

Early in a PhD, much of your methodological training will come through coursework. Later, you will increasingly need to teach yourself new methods, tools, literatures, and ways of thinking.

Learn how you learn best.

Find the right books, papers, tutorials, workshops, courses, and people. Know when to struggle with something yourself and when to ask for help.

The ability to teach yourself something new may be one of the most useful skills you develop during a PhD.

## Build a community of mentors and collaborators

Seek mentors with different lived experiences, disciplinary backgrounds, and methodological expertise.

Not every mentor needs to sit on your committee. Not every important collaborator needs to be at your university.

Try to make these relationships mutually rewarding. Value people's time, experience, and expertise. Give credit when people contribute.

Daniel still meets with old mentors and seeks out new ones. You probably should too.

## Take care of yourself

Productivity requires consistent effort, but overworking without producing meaningful results is neither sustainable nor effective.

Stress can sometimes be useful. Too much stress can become paralyzing. Too little concern about deadlines and responsibilities can also be a warning sign.

Pay attention to your physical and mental health. If something isn't working, change it or ask for help thinking through it.

And sometimes:

> "We missed you, but do you really need to be here? Go take a walk!"

Inspiration often strikes when you're active.

# Keeping This Document Alive

This document should not become a collection of rules that nobody reads.

Some principles here are foundational to the Parker Group and will persist as people come and go. Others should evolve with the group.

Periodically, we should ask:

1. **What should we preserve?**
2. **What isn't currently working or needs clarification?**
3. **What should we add or change given who the lab is now?**
4. **What would we want someone joining the Parker Group next year to understand that isn't captured here?**

When thinking about changes, think about what is good for the group rather than only what works best for you personally.

The goal is not to make everyone work the same way.

The goal is to create a place where different kinds of people can do excellent work together.
